1. The logic: capture what exists, then build what lasts

Knowledge management programs most often fail in one of two ways: they build a repository nobody uses, or they buy a tool before understanding what knowledge actually exists and how it flows. Both failures come from designing before inventorying.

This programme inverts that order. The CI-KM discovery workbook (CI-KM-discovery-workbook_V2.xlsx) first documents the reality on the ground: the business already runs lookbacks, backcasts, post-audits, maturity assessments, and recurring CI reporting - each producing findings, decision rationales, and recommendations. That existing output is the raw material of the enterprise knowledge management capability. The discovery work tells us what it is, where it lives, what it is used for, where it leaks away, and what the business needs the future capability to do.

The build then proceeds component by component, each one grounded in captured evidence rather than assumption - and sequenced so that value appears early (better findability and protected know-how) while the deeper changes (verified follow-up flows, sustained governance) are put in place deliberately.

Guiding principle Decisions change when knowledge reaches them. The capability succeeds when a past lesson changes a plan, a procedure, or a decision - not when a repository fills up.

3. What the enterprise KM capability consists of

The capability is six components. Each is described here in plain language; each exists to fix something the discovery work documented.

1. One managed home for knowledge

A single, searchable place - or a small set of clearly connected places - where lookbacks, backcasts, post-audits, CI reports, lessons, and decision records live. Every item has a name that follows a convention, labels that make it findable (site, topic, decision type, date), and a named owner. Built directly from the Module 1 inventory: the items, their formats, and their current locations tell us exactly what moves in and how it should be organized.

2. Standard capture templates

Simple forms for the recurring artifacts - lessons, decision rationales, recommendations, post-audits - that record the context alongside the conclusion: what was decided or found, what was assumed, what alternatives were considered, what constraints applied, and what happened next. Designed from Module 3's “what's hard to reconstruct later” answers: the context people say goes missing is precisely what the templates make mandatory.

3. Trust labels (quality tiers)

A small set of visible labels that tell a user how much an item can be relied on: approved and validated; reviewed; as-captured (indicative); raw material. Trust labels answer the failure mode of undifferentiated repositories, where users cannot tell the gems from the noise and stop searching. The labels are assigned by rules (review status, source, recency) and displayed wherever items appear.

4. Follow-up flows (closing the loop)

Named, owned routes from a finding to its destination: a lookback finding into the planning model, a validated lesson into a procedure or standard, a recommendation into the next planning cycle, a person's know-how into the system before they leave. Each route ends in verification - evidence that the plan, procedure, or successor actually changed - not merely an action assigned. These flows are drawn from M3's “What happens next,” “Is follow-up checked?,” and “Which closure” columns. The four closures are: finding updates the planning model; lesson becomes a procedure or standard; finding updates the next plan or pathway; a person's know-how is written into the system.

5. The people programme

A standing process for capturing critical know-how before it walks out the door: a risk register of key knowledge holders (from M4, with how big, why, and when), structured interviews and recorded walkthroughs for the highest-risk people, and named receivers for each critical area. Runs continuously, triggered by the register - not by a resignation.

6. Sustainment and scaling

The routines that keep the capability alive after launch: funded owner and curator roles, regular content-health reviews (items past review date, ownerless items, failed searches), usage measurement, and the expansion path from pilot areas to the wider business. Only once content is curated, labeled, and current does AI-based assistance (search, summarization, drafting) get layered on top - see §9.

4. Designing the architecture: from captured data to enterprise search

The discovery modules do more than inventory the present - each one answers a specific design question about the future capability. This section shows how the captured data becomes design decisions, and describes the target architecture in plain language. It is deliberately tool-agnostic: it specifies what each layer must do, not which product or technology should provide it. Tooling is selected later, against these requirements - not before them.

What each module tells the design

Every module in the workbook feeds a specific architecture decision:

ModuleThe design question it answers
M1 ProducedWhat must be ingested: the catalogue of content types, volumes, formats, whether each item is complete, and whether it is safe to reuse
M2 SystemsHow to reach it: one connection method per source; what can be copied out, and what must be read in place
M3 How usedWhat people will search for: the real questions the capability must answer - above all, “what was decided, and what happened next” - and which of the four closures a finding is supposed to land in
M4 PeopleWho validates: the human review loop, and the people dimension of the knowledge record (K- IDs; risk size, reason, and timing)
M5 PainsWhat must work first: the priority searches and use cases, tagged to the eight research gaps
M6 NeedsRequirements and Horizon: what good looks like to the business, and when we would build it (first release, later, or future)

The target architecture in five layers

The target is five layers, each traceable to the modules above. None of them names a tool; each names a job.

LayerIts job
1. IngestionOne connection method per source system. Whatever its origin, every item is normalized into one shared record: title, author, date, source system, a link back to the original, the raw content, a short summary, and tags.
2. DistillationRaw operational content is messy - email threads, shift logs, meeting minutes. A condensation step turns each item into a structured card: what was decided or found, by whom, why, what was expected - and later, what actually happened. The original is always kept and linked, so every summary can be checked against its source.
3. Knowledge layerA small shared vocabulary - the ontology - that names the things the business talks about (decisions, findings, plans, assets) and how they relate. This is what turns a document pile into followable chains: decision, consequence, follow-up.
4. RetrievalThree complementary ways to find things: by exact words, by meaning, and by following links between records. Trust labels (component 3) govern what may be relied on in an answer.
5. AccessTwo doors onto the same governed store: a search page for people, and a machine-readable interface for AI assistants. Permissions and audit are built in from the first day, not added later.

The starter vocabulary for downstream commercial and refining

The ontology starts deliberately small - nine concepts that mirror how the value chain already talks about itself - and grows only when a real search demands more. It is designed from the questions captured in Modules 3 and 5, not in a vacuum; that is what keeps it small and useful.

ConceptWhat it covers
DecisionA choice made at a defined decision point - crude or feedstock selection, a slate change, an inventory policy, a pricing rule, turnaround scope, a logistics routing - recorded with its rationale, assumptions, and expected outcome, and later its actual outcome
Finding / LessonWhat a review concluded - reusable learning, distinguished from one-off observations
Recommendation / ActionThe proposed change that follows from a finding, with an owner and a follow-up status
Review / StudyThe container that produces findings: a lookback, backcast, post-audit, margin bridge, maturity assessment, or CI report
Plan / ModelWhat decisions execute against and findings feed back into - the production plan, the schedule, the demand forecast, the business plan, and their parameters (planning vectors, inventory policies, pricing rules)
Value chain segmentWhere in the chain the item sits: procurement, refining, slate optimization, logistics, or marketing
Site / AssetThe physical anchor: a refinery, a process unit, a terminal or depot
Person / RoleWho decided, produced, reviewed, or owns the follow-up
Standard / ProcedureWhat verified lessons are promoted into - the lesson-to-standard interface made visible

The relationships between these concepts are the point: decided-by, informed-by, applies-to, recorded-in, resulted-in, fed-into, promoted-to, supersedes. They are what let someone ask “show me past decisions on crude slates, and what happened” and receive a chain rather than a pile of files.

Worked example A crude slate decision is recorded as decided-by the supply manager, applying to procurement and refining, recorded-in the decision lookback matrix. A later backcast produces a finding on yield variance; the finding is fed-into the next planning-cycle update; the validated lesson is promoted-to the crude evaluation standard. Every step is a link a person can follow today - and a machine can traverse later.

The consequence loop - the piece that makes “decisions and their consequences” possible

An architecture can only show decisions together with their consequences if consequences are actually recorded. That is why the follow-up flows (component 4) are architectural, not administrative: they write outcomes back against the original decision. The decision record therefore carries two fields completed at different times - expected outcome, filled when the decision is made, and actual outcome, filled when the review happens. Without that loop, the store holds decisions but no consequences - which is precisely the problem the organization has today.

What reference implementations show

External implementations of enterprise knowledge bases - from internal knowledge services at AI companies to open ontology accelerators published by cloud providers - converge on the same principles:

  • Don't fight behavior. Pull knowledge from where work already happens - planning systems, shared drives, email - rather than forcing people onto a new tool first.
  • Distill before indexing. Condense messy raw content into structured summaries; index the summary, and keep the original searchable underneath.
  • Keep the plumbing boring. One shared record shape and proven, unglamorous infrastructure beat clever tooling. Reliability is the feature.
  • Scan, then model, then serve. Discover what exists before designing the vocabulary; model it from real data with human refinement; only then expose search and AI interfaces.
  • Start with a minimum viable vocabulary. A handful of concepts and one high-value question, proven in about ninety days. “Model everything up front” is the classic failure mode.
  • Combine ways of searching. Exact-word, meaning-based, and link-following search each miss things the others catch; the combination is what works.
  • Treat permissions and audit as first-class. Commercially sensitive refinery and trading data demands access control and logging from day one, not as an afterthought.

How the build phases deliver the architecture

The six phases of §5 stay exactly as they are; this mapping shows which layer each phase brings to life:

PhaseArchitecture layer it delivers
1. Organize what existsIngestion and the shared record - prioritized items flow into one governed store (layer 1)
2. Set the standardsThe decision record and the distillation schema - the structured card every item becomes (layer 2)
3. Switch on ownershipTrust labels, permissions, and lifecycle - the governance every other layer relies on (layers 4–5 enablers)
4. Pilot the flowsThe consequence loop - outcomes written back against decisions, starting with the highest-pain loops (layers 2–3)
5. Elicit and expandThe knowledge layer grows - the vocabulary extends only as real questions demand (layer 3)
6. Layer AI assistanceFull retrieval - exact-word, meaning-based, and link-following search over trusted content, plus the machine interface (layers 4–5)

Read together with §2 and §3, this completes the chain: the workbook captures the design inputs, the architecture organizes them into a searchable whole, and the phases deliver it in evidence-gated steps. The promise to the business stays simple: anyone, anywhere in the enterprise, can find what was decided, why, and what happened next - and trust what they find.

5. The build sequence

Six phases, each with an observable gate that must be passed before the next begins. Sequencing by evidence, not by calendar, is what keeps the programme honest.

6. Governance and ownership

The capability is an operating discipline, not a project - its governance is designed to survive leadership rotation and the end of project funding.

  • Executive sponsor. Accountable for the capability's results; sponsorship must be planned to survive rotation (a named deputy, and results embedded in business reviews).
  • Knowledge owners. Every item and knowledge area has a named owner responsible for accuracy, review, and lifecycle decisions. Ownership transfers are part of role handover.
  • Curator role(s). Funded as standing operating cost - not project budget - to run content-health reviews, repair failed searches, chase review dates, and support users.
  • Lifecycle rules. Items move draft → checked → approved → outdated → archived. Review dates plus event triggers (a turnaround, an organizational change, a model update) keep content current.
  • Requirements baseline. The M6 needs, once agreed, become a versioned, change-controlled baseline owned by the sponsor. Design and tool decisions are tested against it. Horizon (first release / later / future) is when we would build, not the workbook fill-order stage.
  • A just, learning-oriented culture. Templates and reviews are framed around systems and decisions, not blame - people record honestly when lessons read as analysis, not accusation.

8. Risks and how this approach handles them

RiskHow the discovery-first approach handles it
“Build it and they won't come” - a repository nobody usesDesign is grounded in Module 3 usage data: the capability is shaped around how people actually use outputs today, and pilots target the pain points people themselves raised
Overwhelming the business with a single giant askCoverage plus six small modules, run in fill-order stages; each stage stands alone and delivers value independently
Tool-first procurement that locks in the wrong shapeRequirements (Module 6) are agreed as outcomes before any tool discussion; tools are selected against the baseline, not before it
Knowledge loss during the programme itselfModule 4 identifies at-risk holders first; elicitation of the highest-risk people is an early priority, not an afterthought
Decay after launch - the most common failure of improvement programsFunded owner/curator roles, management routines, and outcome measurement are components of the design (§6, §7), not optional extras
Templates that feel like blame and suppress honestyCapture formats are explicitly system-framed (what the situation produced), with accountability preserved for conduct - the balance that keeps candor alive

9. The road beyond: AI enablement

AI-based assistance - natural-language search over past lessons, drafted decision records, automatic summarization of reviews - is a deliberate later phase, not a starting point. The reason is simple: AI amplifies whatever state the knowledge base is in. Pointed at scattered, outdated, unlabeled content, it retrieves confidently wrong answers at scale; pointed at a curated, labeled, current corpus, it becomes genuinely useful.

The discovery and build work described here is therefore also the AI-readiness work: the inventory tells the AI what exists, the templates give it structured context, the trust labels tell it what it may rely on, and the governance keeps the corpus current. When AI is introduced, it operates under clear guardrails - it must cite its sources, refuse when evidence is weak, and defer to human approval for anything that changes a plan, a standard, or a public commitment. The organization's judgment, models, and risk appetite remain its own; the AI retrieves, assembles, drafts, and reminds.
