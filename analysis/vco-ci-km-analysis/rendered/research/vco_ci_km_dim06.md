## 1. KEY FINDINGS

**KF1 — Backcasting is a normative, vision-led method with sustainability-transitions roots, now mainstreamed in corporate strategy.** It originated as an alternative to energy forecasting (Robinson, 1982, building on Lovins' 1977 "soft energy paths"), was formalized for organizational sustainability by Holmberg (1998, The Natural Step ABCD four steps), and extended to a five-step participatory method by Quist & Vergragt (2006) whose final step — *embedding and implementation* — is explicitly where most exercises are weakest. Contemporary corporate practice codifies it as: define a "North Star" end state → map required conditions → stage milestones (year 10/5/2) → stress-test against scenarios.[^1^][^2^][^3^][^4^][^5^]

**KF2 — Refinery decarbonization backcasting is structured as a technology-stack pathway to a 2050 net-zero (Scope 1+2) end state.** The canonical stack, in rough marginal-cost order: energy efficiency → electrification of boilers/drives → low-carbon hydrogen (blue first, green later) → CCS on FCC/SMR/heaters → residual offsets/DAC. OGCI/Wood (2023): >65% of a refinery's CO₂ cut by 2040 *if* low-carbon power is available; e-boilers/e-drives <$150/tCO₂ vs ~$500/t for furnace electrification. WEF/IEA tracker instead names CCUS the "primary" pathway and estimates +7–9% refining cost.[^9^][^10^][^12^]

**KF3 — Competing "refinery of the future" end states are all conditional on external systems.** (a) *Fossil-free refinery* (Vogt & Weckhuysen, Nature 2024): CO₂ + waste feedstocks, ~10× the energy input, €14–23bn per site, €320–520bn/yr to replace all 615 refineries over 25+ years — technically argued feasible, economically "staggering". (b) *COTC pivot* (Aramco: 8–12% → up to ~50% chemical yield; TC2C targeting 70–80%; 4 mb/d liquids-to-chemicals by 2030) — but the flagship Yanbu COTC is on hold/re-scoped and COTC carries petrochemical oversupply risk. (c) *Biofuels/SAF conversion* and (d) *closure/import-terminal* round out the observed end-state menu.[^14^][^15^][^16^][^17^][^18^][^20^]

**KF4 — Oil majors build long-term pathways from two distinct instruments that are only loosely coupled:** exploratory scenario planning (Shell tradition since Wack, 1971–72; 2025 Energy Security Scenarios: Archipelagos/Surge exploratory, Horizon explicitly *normative* net-zero-by-2050) and laddered corporate targets (Shell NCI annual steps 2021→2025, 15–20% 2030, 100% 2050; TotalEnergies carbon-intensity steps 15% 2030 / 35% 2040 / 60%+ 2050). Shell itself stresses scenarios are "not forecasts, nor expressions of Shell's strategy or business plan" — an explicit scenario-to-plan gap.[^26^][^27^][^28^][^29^][^30^][^31^][^37^]

**KF5 — Capital allocation is the weakest link between backcast end states and present action.** IEA: industry invested ~USD 20bn in clean energy in 2022 = 2.5% of capex, vs its NZE-consistent benchmark of ~50% of capex to clean energy by 2030 for diversifying producers. TPI Centre (LSE): 98% of oil & gas companies do not align future capex with decarbonization goals; only 2% are 1.5 °C-aligned across 2025/2035/2050. Reclaim Finance: none of the six largest European majors plans >1/3 of capex to "low-carbon"; low-carbon would be at best ~15% of their 2030 energy mix.[^24^][^35^][^39^]

**KF6 — Portfolio/asset backcasting is happening, but is margin- and policy-driven rather than vision-driven.** IEA *Oil 2025*: refined-product demand peaks 2027 at 86.3 mb/d; 4.2 mb/d of new capacity vs 1.6 mb/d announced closures to 2030 — "more capacity will have to shut, with high-cost plants in Europe and on the US West Coast expected to be hardest hit." Wood Mackenzie: Europe loses ~5 mb/d by 2050; by early 2030s half of European refineries show negative net cash margins; least competitive assets must "invest to produce lower carbon, higher value products, or else consider asset closure or conversion."[^23^][^25^]

**KF7 — Refinery conversions are real, staged, and reversible — a live test of backcast-to-execution.** Phillips 66 Rodeo (120 kb/d crude → ~50 kb/d renewable; FID May 2022; commercial ops Apr 2024; SAF from Q3 2024) and Marathon/Neste Martinez (730 MMgy) are the flagship cases; TotalEnergies (La Mède, Grandpuits), Eni (Venice, Livorno — EIB €500m), Repsol (Cartagena, Puertollano) follow in Europe. But Vertex (Mobile, AL) *reverted* its converted unit to fossil; Rodeo ran at reduced rates on 50–55% soybean oil amid weak margins; and environmental-justice critics call conversions "a last-ditch attempt by oil companies to extract the remaining profits from their otherwise stranded billion-dollar assets."[^41^][^42^][^43^][^47^][^46^][^56^]

**KF8 — Interim milestones are systematically fragile.** Shell "retired" its 45% 2035 NCI target in 2024 — just before it entered the 10-year planning window — and weakened 2030 from 20% to 15–20%; its prior legal disclaimer read: "Shell's operating plans cannot reflect our 2050 net-zero emissions target and 2035 NCI target, as these targets are currently outside our planning period." BP moved 40%→25%→abandoned (2030 production-cut target). Carbon Tracker: Shell, Equinor, Occidental have 2050 net-zero goals "but no interim targets." ACCR: Shell's weaker 2030 implies it must "decarbonise up to five times faster between 2030 and 2050."[^32^][^33^][^34^][^36^][^38^]

**KF9 — Pathways are updated episodically by investor pressure, not continuously by evidence.** Follow This: "climate ambitions follow climate votes" — Shell/BP ambition rose with shareholder votes and fell when votes stagnated. This is exactly the failure mode the adaptive-pathways literature (DAPP: signposts, triggers, adaptation tipping points, monitored plan revision) was built to correct; DAPP is standard in climate-adaptation planning but largely absent from refinery transition roadmaps.[^33^][^48^][^49^]

**KF10 — Academic evaluations corroborate the known weaknesses.** Quist/Thissen/Vergragt (2011), reviewing Dutch participatory backcasting 5–10 years on: follow-up and spin-off occurred "but that is not always the case," and mostly at niche level. Geurs & van Wee: generic backcasting suffers "lack of attention for the time-paths for the events between the target years and the base." Climate-delay scholarship (Lamb et al. 2020; Markusson et al. "mitigation deterrence"; Swedish FCCS discourse analysis): end-state storylines "lack specificity in how proposed technologies and measures should be brought about," risking delay of near-term action.[^6^][^7^][^50^]

**KF11 — Co-processing is the archetypal "low-regret intermediate step" and shows how regulation gates the pathway.** ASTM D1655 caps lipid co-processing at 5 vol% (and 24% feed / 10% product for pre-hydrotreated streams); ASTM is evaluating raising 5%→30%. Low ratios need minimal capex; higher ratios drive hydrogen, corrosion, and catalyst-inactivation costs — i.e., the backcast pathway's early milestone is cheap, but the slope steepens.[^21^][^22^]

**KF12 — Implication for VCO/CI knowledge management:** the research consensus is that backcasting fails at the *interface* with operational planning (under-specified intermediate steps, targets outside planning periods, capex misalignment, no monitored signposts). A downstream VCO program should therefore treat the backcast pathway as a *living artifact*: milestone owners inside the planning horizon, trigger/signpost metrics, scheduled lookback reviews that re-baseline the pathway (double-loop learning), and retention of pathway assumptions and evidence in the CI knowledge base. (Synthesis; see Section 6.)

## 2. EVIDENCE — Topic 1: Strategic backcasting methodology (origins; vs forecasting)

### 2.1 Origins in energy/sustainability transitions literature

> "Backcasting was originally developed by Robinson (1982) as an alternative to conventional energy planning that predominantly relied upon forecasting methods. It took inspiration from Lovin's (1977) work that sought to describe alternative energy futures … as forms of 'soft energy paths', put in contrast to mainstream developments that extrapolated past and previous trends into the future…" [^3^]

> "A key feature of backcasting is its acknowledgement of futures as not only a matter of prediction and likelihood based on developments of the present and the past, but also a matter of feasibility and choice (Robinson, 1988, 1990…)." [^3^]

> "Backcasting was first introduced in the energy field as an alternative to traditional forecasting (Robinson, 1982). The main characteristic of the approach is that focus is not on how to adapt to a likely future, but on how the most desirable future can be attained. In that way, strategies are designed 'backwards', from the future to the present." [^1^]

### 2.2 Definition and contrast with forecasting

> "…is a method in, which the future desired conditions are envisioned and steps are then defined to attain those conditions, rather than to take steps that are merely a continuum of present methods extrapolated into the future." — Holmberg & Robèrt (2000, p. 294), quoted in [^2^]

> "Backcasting is a technique that addresses 'the articulation of desired futures, and the analysis of how they might be achieved' [12, p. 1]." — review of Robinson in [^5^]

When to use it — Dreborg's (1996, p. 816) canonical applicability criteria:

> "the problem to be studied is complex,… there is a need for major change,… dominant trends are part of the problem,… the problem to a great extent is a matter of externalities,… the scope is wide enough and the time horizon long enough to leave considerable room for deliberate choice…" [^1^]

Forecasting vs backcasting as complementary lenses (consultancy synthesis):

> "Forecasting is a forward projection… asking: *Where will our current path take us?* Backcasting is reverse-looking based on a desired end state… asking: *What must we do now to reach our desired destination?*" … "Relying solely on forecasting in a volatile market leads to the Incumbent's Trap… Conversely, randomly applied backcasting will lead to goals that are disconnected from today's business…" [^4^]

### 2.3 Canonical step sequences

**Holmberg (1998) four steps (The Natural Step / ABCD):**
> "1. Defining criteria for sustainability; 2. Describing the current situation, in relation to the criteria for sustainability; 3. Envisioning the future; 4. Finding strategies for sustainability" [^1^]

**Quist & Vergragt (2006) five-step participatory framework:**
> "…strategic problem orientation, normative future image, backcasting, elaboration and analysis, and embedding and implementation." [^5^] (Elsewhere rendered: "1) strategic problem orientation, 2) future vision development, 3) backcasting analysis, 4) future alternatives and follow-up agenda, and 5) results and agenda embedment and follow-up stimulation." [^8^])

**Contemporary corporate mechanics (consultancy codification):**
> "1. **Define the future state**. Set a concrete, ambitious endpoint … typically 10 to 20 years out. This is often referred to as the **North Star**… 2. **Map the conditions required.** … What technologies must exist? What regulations must be in place? … 3. **Identify the milestones.** … what needs to be in place by year 10, year 5, year 2… 4. **Stress-test against uncertainty.** Run the roadmap through different scenarios…" [^4^]

Sector application example (zero-emission shipping, Frontiers in Marine Science 2025):
> "Backcasting sets the green vision and goals for the future shipping industry from a long-term perspective, and then identifies the institutions, policies, and technologies necessary to realize them." [^51^]

**Note on method diversity:** the literature stresses there is no single standard: "the outlined approach should not be seen as the general or standard approach for backcasting studies, but rather a single backcasting method among others" (Geurs & van Wee, cited in [^6^]). This diversity is a strength for tailoring but undermines comparability and auditability of corporate backcasts.

## 3. EVIDENCE — Topic 2: Net-zero / decarbonization backcasting for refiners

### 3.1 OGCI refining decarbonization roadmap (electrification-led)

OGCI (12 CEO-led majors) commissioned Wood plc to study electrification of three representative refineries; published October 2023:

> "The study's primary roadmap showed that it would be possible to reduce over 65% of a refinery's CO2 by 2040 if there was sufficient availability of low-carbon power. It found that using electric boilers and electric drives for condensing turbines should be prioritized. These proven technologies have marginal abatement costs under $150 per tonne of CO2 – significantly cheaper than the marginal abatement costs of $500/t of CO2 to convert furnaces to electric heaters." [^9^]

> "'Decarbonizing the refining sector, which emits around 1 gigatonne of CO2e a year, will play an important role in achieving a net zero emissions future and our latest study on the refinery sector sets out pathways to support that goal.' — Justine Roure, OGCI Deputy Vice President Strategy & Policy" [^9^]

OGCI's 2024 Progress Report shows the pathway being *extended* (evidence that at least this industry roadmap is iterated):

> "- Identified potential pathways to significantly reduce emissions in the refining sector by using low-carbon electricity instead of fossil fuels to power some equipment; - Is following up work on refinery emissions reduction pathways with a study on the economics of refinery electrification, assessing operating expenditures considerations and the influence of power, fuel and carbon pricing; - Will study heat pump applications… across upstream, midstream and downstream…" [^10^]

The Oil & Gas Decarbonization Charter (OGDC, COP28) sets the *end state* these roadmaps serve:

> "These ambitions include working toward net-zero operations by 2050, near-zero upstream methane emissions, and zero routine flaring by 2030, in addition to measuring and publicly reporting progress…" — 54 companies, ~45% of global oil production [^52^]

### 3.2 IEA / WEF pathway framing

WEF Net-Zero Industry Tracker 2023 (with IEA/MPP data):

> "CCUS is the primary decarbonization pathway for refineries, particularly for reducing emissions from burning waste fuel gases and pet coke. Refinery hydrogen production units generate a sufficiently pure stream of CO2, making carbon capture suitable. Additionally, emerging technologies like clean hydrogen and electrification of heat and power sources offer potential decarbonization alternatives, albeit at early stages of development." [^12^]

> "…decarbonizing refinery operations through CCUS and clean hydrogen, where possible. Energy efficiency will also be vital." … "However, refining decarbonization measures, including CCUS and clean hydrogen, are still in their early stages and are expected to raise refining costs by 7-9%." [^12^]

**Tension flagged:** OGCI/Wood leads with electrification (cheap, proven); WEF/IEA leads with CCUS (highest-abatement, especially on hydrogen plants); CATF (below) treats them as complementary site-specific options. Sequencing and lead technology in refinery backcasts is *not* settled — it depends on regional power prices, CO₂ transport/storage access, and carbon price.

IEA's framing for company-level pathways (The Oil and Gas Industry in Net Zero Transitions):

> "The oil and gas industry invested around USD 20 billion in clean energy in 2022, some 2.5% of its total capital spending. … our bottom-up analysis of cash flows in a 1.5 °C scenario suggests that a reasonable ambition is for 50% of capital expenditures to go towards clean energy projects by 2030, on top of the investment needed to reduce scope 1 and 2 emissions." [^24^]

> "Not all oil and gas companies have to diversify into clean energy, but the alternative is to wind down traditional operations over time." [^24^]

### 3.3 Site-level roadmapping practice (Worley / Refining India 2024) — what a refinery backcast actually looks like

A two-phase scope-1+2 net-zero-by-2050 study shows the standard engineering method (screen → rank → schedule):

> "A total of 42 options were identified, including direct abatement measures, carbon capture and storage technologies, switching to low-carbon fuels, CO₂ valorisation, and GHG offsets. … The top 20 options were selected for further detailed evaluation in Phase 2." [^11^]

Key projects: grey-to-blue hydrogen conversion; CCS on cogeneration and boilers; hydrogen pinch/recovery; utility-system optimization; biomass boiler fuel-switch; low-carbon hydrogen heaters; green hydrogen import; CO₂-to-methanol and CO₂-to-SAF valorisation — the last two:

> "…were found to be economically unattractive at present due to future price trends of products and raw materials. However, it is recommended to perform market studies at appropriate times as specified in the roadmap timeline." [^11^]

Analyst note: this is a rare example of an explicit *re-evaluation trigger embedded in a roadmap timeline* — precisely the mechanism most backcasts lack (see Topic 5).

### 3.4 CATF "Refinery of the Future" (2024) — archetype pathways across regions

> "The Refinery of the Future report examines the technical pathways that existing refineries could use to decarbonize their operations during a transition away from traditional transportation fuels… The pathways include: Energy Efficiency… Carbon Capture and Storage… for hard-to-abate process emissions from the fluidized catalytic cracker (FCC) and the crude distillation unit (CDU)… Low-Carbon Hydrogen… New technologies: …electric-powered furnaces (e-furnaces) and oxy-firing… currently at low levels of technical readiness…" Regions: Europe, US Gulf Coast, Middle East GCC, Singapore. [^13^]

CATF's archetype modeling makes the closure-vs-decarbonize logic of portfolio backcasting explicit:

> "…underutilising a refinery, as in Case 1, is not considered to be a financially feasible option; instead, it is expected that some refineries will shut down to allow for maximum throughput operations in the remaining refineries." [^13^]

> "Whilst some refineries may turn down operations by 2030 as they prepare to divest from the current portfolio towards biorefineries or other clean fuels facilities, others will be profitable and essential enough to the market to continue operation up to and beyond 2050… For these refineries, the aim would be to maximize throughput, and decarbonise Scope 1 & 2 emissions through green hydrogen uptake, carbon capture, electrification, and increased energy efficiency." [^13^]

### 3.5 Academic end-state: Vogt & Weckhuysen, "The refinery of the future" (Nature, May 2024)

> "We can attempt to close the carbon cycle associated with their use by electrifying refinery processes and by changing the raw materials that go into a refinery from fossils fuels to carbon dioxide for making hydrocarbon fuels and to agricultural and municipal waste for making chemicals and polymers. We argue that, with sufficient long-term commitment and support, the science and technology for such a completely fossil-free refinery, delivering the products required after 2050 (less fuels, more chemicals), could be developed. This future refinery will require substantially larger areas and greater mineral resources than is the case at present and critically depends on the capacity to generate large amounts of renewable energy for hydrogen production and carbon dioxide capture." [^14^]

Scale/cost (Utrecht University release):

> "A new refinery requires investments in the order of 14 to 23 billion euros… Just replacing all 615 refineries now operating over the next 25 plus years would require investments on the order of 320 to 520 billion euros per year." … "The entire process would need to be powered by renewable energy — and is estimated to require ten times as much energy as existing refineries require." [^15^][^16^]

Nature's editorial captures the credibility question idealized end states face:

> "Vogt and Weckhuysen set out a blueprint for decarbonizing oil-refinery capacity by mid-century. They acknowledge that the cost and scale of the necessary transition are 'staggering'. Their vision deserves attention. Industry leaders and policymakers need to take it seriously." [^16^]

### 3.6 Petrochemical integration / COTC as a demand-side backcast end state

Aramco (company page):

> "…tweaking existing, proven technologies and processes in an integrated refining complex to raise the chemical production level per barrel of oil from the regular 8% to 12% yield, to up to around 50%." [^17^]

> "Aramco's global liquids-to-chemicals programme aims to convert 4 million barrels a day (b/d) of its oil production into high-value petrochemicals and chemical feedstocks by 2030." [^20^]

Nexant on the COTC investment wave and its risk:

> "Growing appetite in transitioning from fuels to petrochemicals has attracted almost US$ 120 billion in investments in COTC facilities globally, where more than 150 million tons per year of refining capacity either planned or operating." … "In Saudi Arabia, originally planned for a startup in 2025, Saudi Aramco and SABIC have delayed firm timings for COTC investments…" [^18^]

> "COTC technology allows the direct conversion of crude oil to high-value chemical products… exceeding 70% to 80% of the barrel producing chemical feedstock as opposed to ~10% in a non-integrated refinery complex." [^19^]

**Tension flagged:** COTC is a *demand-hedging* end state (fuels → chemicals), not a decarbonization one; it can raise site emissions (CATF Case 2: +2% emissions for 5× naphtha) and faces petrochemical oversupply (Asia aromatics; European cracker closures — IEA: European petrochemical feedstock consumption −19% 2019–2024 [^23^]). The Yanbu flagship's "On Hold" status [^20^] shows even NOC backcasts slip against commercial reality.

### 3.7 Biofuels/SAF co-processing — the near-term, low-capex rung

> "Co-hydroprocessing of oils and fats, and Fischer-Tropsch liquids are allowed at a maximum of 5%, while previously hydrotreated fats and oils can be inserted at 24%, although the final biogenic fraction in the jet fuel may not be more than 10%. ASTM is currently evaluating increasing the 5% limit to 30%…" [^21^]

> "Commercial experience has indicated that co-processing lipids at low ratios (e.g. 5%) has a relatively minor impact on refinery operations and the associated risks can be managed with limited investment in new and modified infrastructure. However, as co-processing ratios increase, it is likely that much greater investment will be required and more extensive refinery modifications will be needed." (Risks: "fuel quality issues, corrosion of metallurgy, inactivation of catalysts, increased hydrogen demand") [^21^]

> "…existing hydrotreaters or hydrocrackers can be adapted for co-processing with minimal upgrades. The most common modifications involve changing catalysts… For blending ratios up to 5% renewable content, these changes are typically straightforward and do not require major capital investment." [^22^]

Regulatory gating (EU): "EU SAF must be produced from feedstocks that are listed in Annex IX Part A and B of RED III… the EU restricts the use of food and feed crops for biofuels and completely prohibits their use for SAF production." [^53^]

### 3.8 IRENA framing (sector-agnostic end-state logic applied to fuels)

IRENA, *Reaching Zero with Renewables* (2020), distinguishes direct vs indirect electrification — the logic that underlies refinery electrification-first roadmaps:

> "Direct electrification is usually to be preferred over indirect electrification, given that it is an overall simpler, cheaper and more efficient approach… Indirect electrification of end-use sectors will require changes across the value chain, from production to consumption, which results in higher costs and lower efficiencies." [^54^]

IRENA also notes advanced biofuels meet only ~0.004% of global jet fuel demand today — quantifying how far the SAF end state is from present capability. [^54^]

## 4. EVIDENCE — Topic 3: How oil companies build long-term pathways (scenarios, targets, milestones, capital allocation)

### 4.1 The Shell scenarios tradition (exploratory foresight)

OECD (2003) on the origins:

> "Scenario planning started at Shell in the early 1970s, with the work of Pierre Wack, who was able to present Shell's board of directors with two scenarios - one titled 'conventional wisdom' and the other titled 'oil price crisis scenario'. These were made available only months before the October 1973 oil crisis, thus preparing the company for that abrupt change. … its first scenario that dared look out over 50 years was produced in 1995." … "In general the Shell scenarios are of the 'exploratory' type, designed around 'what if' questions…" [^26^]

Wack's purpose was cognitive, not predictive:

> "Scenarios deal with two worlds; the world of facts and the world of perceptions. They explore for facts but they aim at perceptions inside the heads of decision-makers." — Pierre Wack, quoted in [^27^]

> "…the executives who engaged with these scenarios developed what Wack called 'prepared minds': they could recognize the 1973 oil crisis when it arrived and respond faster than competitors still trapped in old assumptions." [^27^]

Methodological caveat (from the scenario literature itself): Wack rejected probability-weighted scenarios and single-point forecasts — "he presented scenarios as an alternative planning method to what he saw as an over-reliance on numerical forecasts." [^55^]

### 4.2 Shell 2025/2026 Energy Security Scenarios — normative backcasting enters the canon

> "In Shell's Energy Security Scenarios 2025, both Archipelagos (a fragmented world driven by security concerns) and Surge (a world where AI accelerates the pace of economic growth) are exploratory scenarios, while Horizon is a normative scenario that aims to deliver a net-zero energy system by 2050." [^29^]

> "**Horizon** – illustrative of a rapid acceleration of the energy transition and introduction of carbon management practices to sharply reduce emissions… The scenario takes a normative approach aimed at a world that achieves two key things: net-zero emissions by 2050 and global warming limited to 1.5°C by the end of the century." [^28^]

Horizon's built-in honesty about the 2030 gap: "CO2 emissions in Horizon peak in 2025, but by 2030 the reduction compared to 2010 is ~7%. By design, the scenario reaches net-zero CO2 by 2050." — i.e., even Shell's normative scenario does not deliver the Glasgow 45%-by-2030 milestone. [^56^]

2026 scenarios update (evidence of scenario refresh cadence): "The 2026 Energy Security Scenarios indicate net-zero CO2 emissions is likely, but timing is uncertain. Plausible scenarios indicate a near-term overshoot of the 1.5°C goal." [^30^]

The explicit scenario–strategy firewall:

> "While they are not forecasts, nor are they expressions of Shell's strategy or business plan, they are designed to help business leaders and policymakers explore and plan." [^57^]

### 4.3 Target-setting with laddered milestones (as stated)

Shell's public target ladder (company page, accessed 2025–26):

> "- Halving Scope 1 and 2 emissions under our operational control by 2030, on a net basis, compared with 2016… - Reducing the net carbon intensity (NCI) of the products we sell by 15-20% by 2030… We have set short-, medium- and long-term targets… 2-3% by 2021 - achieved; 3-4% by 2022 - achieved; 6-8% by 2023 - achieved; 9-12% by 2024 - achieved; 9-13% by 2025; 15-20% by 2030; 100% by 2050." [^31^]

TotalEnergies' ladder (via investor briefing):

> "60% or more reduction in the average carbon intensity of energy products used worldwide by Total customers by 2050 (less than 27.5 gCO2/MJ) – with intermediate steps of 15% by 2030 and 35% by 2040 (scope 1 + 2 + 3). Total currently allocates more than 10% of its Capex to low carbon electricity [and 20% in 2030], the highest level among the Majors." [^37^]

### 4.4 The milestone-revision record (targets weakened as they approach the planning horizon)

Shell Energy Transition Strategy 2024:

> "Shell has abandoned a key climate target for 2035 and weakened another goal for 2030… The oil major has 'updated' its target to cut the total 'net carbon intensity' of all the energy products it sells… by 20% between 2016 and 2030. The reduction is now set at between 15-20%. … Shell notes that it has 'chosen to retire [its] 2035 target of a 45% reduction in net carbon intensity' due to 'uncertainty in the pace of change in the energy transition'." [^32^]

> "'It's very telling that Shell scraps its 2035 target in 2024, just before it moves into its ten-year planning period. This begs the question: what is the value of the 2050 net-zero target, when Shell can scrap it before it becomes pertinent?' … Shell always stated in the legal disclaimer of its Climate Target: 'Shell's operating plans cannot reflect our 2050 net-zero emissions target and 2035 NCI target, as these targets are currently outside our planning period.'" [^33^]

Shell's spokesperson rationale: "'When we provided the 2035 target, it was assumed the world would develop an accounting system for tracking carbon emissions. That has not happened which means we cannot track the 2035 target today.'" [^58^]

BP's sequence (40% → 25% → abandoned):

> "BP in February said it planned a 25% cut in oil and gas output by 2030, stepping back from a previous goal of a 40% reduction. … According to Reuters, BP CEO Murray Auchincloss is resetting BP's energy transition strategy, instead looking to boost its oil and gas output through new investments in the Middle East and the Gulf of Mexico. … It is sticking with its goal to achieve net zero emissions by 2050, the report said." [^34^]

> "'As Murray said at the start of year in our fourth-quarter results, the direction is the same—but we are going to deliver as a simpler, more focused, and higher-value company,' a BP spokesperson said." [^34^]

Reclaim Finance on the 2023 European-major reset:

> "In 2023, some companies scaled back their already poor climate ambitions. For instance, BP significantly decreased its oil and gas production reduction target, first from a 40% reduction by 2030 to only a 25% reduction, and then more recently completely inverted the objective by announcing that its production will increase. In the same year, Shell dropped plans to cut its oil production by 2030, instead projecting a plateau. More recently, TotalEnergies used its investment [day] in September 2024 to announce the company's new aim to increase both its oil and gas production by 3% per year until 2028…" [^35^]

Follow This's mechanism diagnosis:

> "Shell's retreat from climate targets demonstrates that climate ambitions follow climate votes, both positively and negatively… as votes increased, so did Shell's climate ambitions. When votes fell and remained stagnant, climate ambitions were abandoned…" [^33^]

### 4.5 Independent assessments of pathway credibility

Carbon Tracker "Absolute Impact" (hallmarks of Paris-aligned targets):

> "They must: cover the full life-cycle emissions of their products, including use (Scopes 1, 2 and 3); target net zero by 2050 on a full life-cycle basis, setting interim targets for absolute reductions along the way; cover worldwide emissions from all projects… **Shell, Equinor and Occidental Petroleum… have 2050 net zero goals covering full life-cycle emissions, but no interim targets.** … **Aramco**, in last place, is the only company to limit emissions reductions targets to assets that it wholly owns and operates. It has set no baselines, pledging only to reduce emissions against amounts forecast under business-as-usual scenarios." [^38^]

Transition Pathway Initiative Centre (LSE, 2023):

## 5. EVIDENCE — Topic 4: Backcasting for portfolio/asset configuration (conversions, closures, regional repositioning)

### 5.1 The structural demand backdrop (IEA Oil 2025) — the "future state" refiners backcast from

> "Global refined products demand is projected to peak in 2027 at 86.3 mb/d, only 710 kb/d above 2024 levels. Thereafter, accelerating declines in gasoline and diesel use outweigh growth in naphtha and jet fuel. Despite tepid demand growth projections, 4.2 mb/d of new refining capacity is expected globally by 2030, partly offset by 1.6 mb/d of closures. Even so, net capacity growth is set to far exceed refined product demand… This indicates that more capacity will have to shut, with high-cost plants in Europe and on the US West Coast expected to be hardest hit." [^23^]

> "In the 2024-26 period, cumulative refinery closures for these regions [Europe, OECD Asia Oceania, US] total 1.3 mb/d. We only include announced closures in our forecasts, but lower regional processing rates in this Report reflect the risk that further closures are likely. The European Fuel Manufacturers Association's (CONCAWE) recent study… suggests a reduction of 2.1 mb/d of CDU capacity in Europe by 2030 is possible." [^23^]

> "In 2025 alone, the United States is set to close over 400 kb/d of capacity. On the Gulf Coast, LyondellBasell's 265 kb/d Houston refinery permanently shut in Q1 2025 after efforts to find a buyer proved unsuccessful. … Phillips 66 shuttered its 120 kb/d Rodeo refinery in early 2024 [converted to renewables] and will close its 140 kb/d Wilmington facility in Q4 2025 as part of a broader portfolio optimisation strategy. Further losses are expected next year, with Valero's 145 kb/d Benicia refinery slated for shutdown and repurposing in H1 2026 amid intensifying environmental compliance costs and regulatory pressure." [^23^]

Europe 2025 closures: "Shell has closed the 150 kb/d Wesseling section of the Rheinland-Palatinate refinery in Germany, BP is set to shut an 80 kb/d unit at its 240 kb/d Gelsenkirchen refinery before year-end and the 140 kb/d Petroineos Grangemouth facility in the United Kingdom was shuttered in April this year. Several planned biofuel conversions, such as Grandpuits, Wesseling and Venice, are proceeding, but these involve shutting traditional CDU capacity… as well as conversion capacity which cuts light product yields." [^23^]

> "This signals a structural transition in refining — from volumetric growth to value, improved flexibility, emissions mitigation and optimising product yield." … "By 2026 East of Suez refining capacity will exceed the Atlantic Basin for the first time." [^23^]

### 5.2 Consultancy view: adaptation as a margin-led sorting process (Wood Mackenzie)

> "Global refinery crude throughput peaks in the late 2020s in our outlook… Looking ahead beyond 2030, in Europe, falling demand and the negative impact on margins will force more refinery closures in the 2030s, and Europe is forecast to lose as much as 5 mb/d of capacity by 2050. North America follows a similar trend." [^25^]

> "In fact, by the early 2030s half the refineries in Europe will be generating negative net cash margins… The least competitive assets will need to invest to produce lower carbon, higher value products, or else consider asset closure or conversion." [^25^]

### 5.3 Flagship conversions — the staged execution of a portfolio backcast

**Phillips 66 Rodeo (CA):** announced Aug 2020 ("Rodeo Renewed"), FID May 2022, commercial renewable-diesel ops Apr 2024, SAF from Q3 2024.

> "Phillips 66 on April 1 announced that its Rodeo refinery in San Francisco has commenced commercial production of renewable diesel. … The converted facility, referred to as the Rodeo Renewable Energy Complex, is now processing only renewable feedstocks and producing approximately 30,000 barrels per day of renewable diesel… on track to ramp up production rates to nameplate capacity of more than 800 MMgy (50,000 barrels per day)…" [^42^]

> "The biorefinery project has been under development since mid-2022 and reached full processing rates of approximately 50,000 barrels per day during the second quarter of 2024. … the Rodeo facility has been running approximately 50% to 55% soybean oil, but plans to increase the share of lower CI feedstocks… SAF production will begin during the third quarter…" [^43^]

Execution risk visible within 2 years of startup: "Phillips 66… reporting the company's Rodeo Renewable Energy Complex in California continues to run at reduced rates due to market conditions." [^47^] And: "U.S. renewable diesel production capacity has increased sixfold since 2020, but future production will be far below capacity unless the federal government significantly expands policy support." [^60^]

**Marathon/Neste Martinez (CA):** idled 2020, conversion approved March 2021 (50/50 JV with Neste, ~$1.2bn), production from early 2023, fire late 2023, ramping to 730 MMgy nameplate. [^41^][^61^]

**Europe:** TotalEnergies La Mède biorefinery and Grandpuits conversion; Eni Venice (operating) and Livorno — "The EIB and Eni on July 24 signed a €500 million 15-year finance contract to support the conversion of Eni's Livorno refinery in Tuscany, Italy, into a biorefinery that can produce renewable diesel, renewable naphtha and bio-LPG." [^47^] Repsol Cartagena (250 kt/y, Apr 2024) and Puertollano. [^62^] Grangemouth: closed April 2025, converting to fuels import terminal; OPIS: "Wood Mackenzie expects European crude capacity to shrink over 300,000 b/d due to refinery conversions and closures." [^44^]

**Reversal case (evidence that conversions are not one-way commitment devices):**

> "Vertex Energy Inc. on May 9 announced it will cease renewable diesel production at its refinery in Mobile, Alabama, and return the previously converted hydrocracker unit to fossil fuel production later this year." [^47^]

### 5.4 The critique of conversion-led end states

Environmental Integrity Project (2024), quoting Communities for a Better Environment:

> "Guerin said biofuel conversions like Rodeo's are, 'best understood as a last-ditch attempt by oil companies to extract the remaining profits from their otherwise stranded billion-dollar assets and kick the can down the road. They are postponing the day they have to decommission and, critically, remediate these sites…'" … "according to the project's own environmental impact report, the differences in pollution between petroleum and biofuels refining is 'small,' with some hazardous pollutants actually increasing…" [^46^]

Policy dependence: conversions are economically anchored to the US RFS/LCFS/45Z credits and California market — "capacity expansion has outpaced the policies that renewable diesel relies on to be profitable." [^60^]

### 5.5 Regional repositioning end states

- **China:** capacity ~18.5→19.5 mb/d by 2030 with a policy "capacity ceiling near 20 mb/d"; growth via "complex, integrated refinery-petrochemical (crude-to-chemicals) hubs… balanced by the closure of small, less efficient operations" (Yulong 400 kb/d, Gulei II 320 kb/d). [^23^]
- **India:** +1.3 mb/d to 14.3 mb/d regional (India ~1 mb/d of additions; Panipat +200 kb/d 2026; Barmer greenfield 180 kb/d 2026). [^23^]
- **Middle East:** +860 kb/d product supply by 2030 consolidating export-hub role; S-Oil Shaheen (Aramco TC2C, ~70% chemical conversion), Amiral (Aramco/TotalEnergies, $11bn). [^23^][^18^]
- **Aramco corporate strategy:** liquids-to-chemicals to 4 mb/d by 2030; downstream acquisitions (Gdansk 30%, SABIC 70%) — repositioning toward chemicals and Asian demand centers. [^20^][^63^]
- **OECD Asia Oceania:** "New Zealand has exited refining entirely"; Japan/Korea shifting "toward petrochemical integration, energy efficiency and alternative fuels." [^23^]

Analyst note: the observed portfolio behavior matches a *backcast* logic (position the asset base for a 2030–2050 demand state) but the *drivers cited by companies and agencies are margin, carbon cost (EU ETS free-allowance phase-out: −20% by 2025, >−40% by 2030 [^23^]), and policy credits — i.e., portfolio backcasting is implicit and price-led, not an explicit vision-led exercise with published intermediate milestones.*

## 6. EVIDENCE — Topic 5: Known weaknesses of backcasting (academic + observed)

### 6.1 Weakness A — Follow-up/embedding is the weakest step (academic evaluations)

Quist, Thissen & Vergragt (2011) — the only systematic 5–10-year follow-up study of participatory backcasting:

> "This paper reports on a study that has systematically investigated the follow-up and spin-off of participatory backcasting experiments in the Netherlands five to ten years after completion. … The cases show that participatory backcasting can lead to substantial follow-up and spin-off, **but that is not always the case**. Substantial follow-up and spin-off after five to ten years is predominantly found at the level of niches, and can be seen as potential seeds for future system innovations." [^7^]

Under-specification of time-paths (Geurs & van Wee, via systematic review):

> "…the approach seems quite mild, just as its predecessors, it however has a shortcoming of **lack of attention for the time-paths for the events between the target years and the base**." [^6^]

### 6.2 Weakness B — Idealized / politically shaped end states

- Industry end states are set by incumbents: OGDC ambitions ("net-zero operations by 2050… zero routine flaring by 2030") are framed by 54 producer companies themselves [^52^]; OGCI's refining roadmap is a member-led study by 12 majors [^9^]. Scope boundary (Scope 1+2) excludes the ~85–90% of life-cycle emissions in Scope 3 — Carbon Tracker's first "hallmark" requires full life-cycle coverage, which only Eni fully met (and Eni's plan is questioned for reliance on asset sales, CCS and offsets). [^38^]
- Normative scenarios can serve as delay devices. Climate-delay scholarship: Lamb et al. (2020) identify four discourses of delay ("Surrender," "Redirect responsibility," "Push non-transformative solutions," "Emphasize the downsides"); mitigation deterrence = "the prospect of reduced or delayed mitigation resulting from the introduction or consideration of another climate intervention" (Markusson et al., 2018). Swedish parliamentary FCCS storyline analysis: storylines "are both adamant that emissions must be reduced yet **lack specificity in how proposed technologies and measures should be brought about**… Taken together, the lack of specificity found in the discourse and the confidence in the success of these technologies constitute a risk of delaying more ambitious action to reduce emissions in the short term." [^50^]
- "Net" end states hide accounting choices: intensity targets "can be met whilst absolute emissions increase" (ACCR) [^36^]; divestment counted as reduction [^36^]; offsets scale — Shell retired 17.3 million credits in 2024 [^64^].

### 6.3 Weakness C — Interim milestones collapse at the planning-horizon boundary

See Section 4.4 evidence: Shell's 2035 target retired just before entering the 10-year planning window; BP's 40→25→abandoned sequence; Carbon Tracker: majors with 2050 goals but "no interim targets" [^38^]; ACCR: weakened 2030 implies 5× faster post-2030 decarbonization with "little guidance" on how [^36^]; targets set "when the managers that set them are unlikely to be held accountable" [^40^]. ICAO/ICSA makes the same point in aviation: "'If you plot those two points on a graph, what happens in between? Unless [ICAO] identifies a pathway, how can it assess the effectiveness of its tools…?'" [^65^]

### 6.4 Weakness D — Pathways not updated as evidence accumulates

- Corporate updates are *episodic and investor-driven*, not monitoring-driven: "climate ambitions follow climate votes" [^33^]; BP's 2023 reset was justified on "energy security" grounds [^66^]; Shell's ETS24 on "value over volume." [^32^]
- Even Shell's own scenario practice now concedes overshoot: 2026 scenarios "indicate a near-term overshoot of the 1.5 °C goal" [^30^] — an evidence update absorbed at scenario level but with no mechanism forcing re-derivation of corporate milestones.
- **Corrective template exists (DAPP) but is absent from refinery roadmaps.** Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (Haasnoot et al., 2013): "Signposts specify the information that should be monitored to determine whether the plan is still meeting the conditions for its success… Triggers are the critical values of signpost variables beyond which additional actions or measures should be taken." [^48^] "The plan 'covers short-term investment decisions, long-term options, and adaptation signals to identify when to implement actions or revisit decisions and consider alternate pathways'" (Haasnoot et al., 2020, via [^49^]). Adaptation pathways explicitly "provide insight into the sequencing of actions over time, potential lock-ins, and path dependencies." [^49^]
- Closest refinery-sector analog found: the Worley roadmap's instruction to "perform market studies at appropriate times as specified in the roadmap timeline" for CO₂-valorisation options [^11^], and OGCI's staged follow-on studies (electrification economics, heat pumps) [^10^]. These are exceptions.

### 6.5 Weakness E — Weak linkage to operational planning and CI

- The Shell disclaimer is the canonical artifact: "Shell's operating plans cannot reflect our 2050 net-zero emissions target and 2035 NCI target, as these targets are currently outside our planning period." [^33^] i.e., the backcast end state is *formally disconnected* from the operating plan.
- Capex-level disconnection: 98% of O&G/electricity companies do not align future capex with long-term decarbonization goals; 92% do not quantify key elements of their strategy (TPI). [^39^]
- Asset-level reality: closures are driven by margin and compliance cost, not by pathway milestones (IEA: Wilmington closed "as part of a broader portfolio optimisation strategy"; Benicia "amid intensifying environmental compliance costs" [^23^]); conversions run at reduced rates when credits/margins weaken [^47^][^60^]; one conversion reversed to fossil [^47^].
- Backcasting method guidance itself warns against this: "randomly applied backcasting will lead to goals that are disconnected from today's business, emerging technological, political, regulatory, ecological, or social realities." [^4^]

## 7. TENSIONS AND CONTRADICTIONS (cross-cutting)

1. **Lead technology contested:** OGCI/Wood → electrification first (<$150/t) [^9^]; WEF/IEA → "CCUS is the primary decarbonization pathway" [^12^]; CATF → portfolio with CCS on FCC/SMR + low-carbon H₂, e-furnaces too immature to count [^13^]; Vogt & Weckhuysen → full feedstock reinvention (CO₂ + waste) at ~10× energy demand [^14^]. Sequencing is region- and policy-dependent, not universal.
2. **Normative end state vs. commercial behavior:** majors retain net-zero 2050 while raising near-term production (BP, Shell plateau, TotalEnergies +3%/yr to 2028) [^34^][^35^]; Shell's own normative Horizon scenario reaches only ~7% below-2010 CO₂ by 2030 [^56^] — the "milestone gap" is embedded even in the vision documents.
3. **IEA NZE "no new oil and gas projects" vs. industry demand view:** Carbon Brief: Shell's "Investment in oil and gas will be needed…" is "a bet against the world meeting its climate goals"; IEA WEO 2023 says underinvestment warnings "are no longer based on the latest technology and market trends" and risks are "weighted more towards overinvestment." [^32^]
4. **COTC as transition strategy vs. petrochemical oversupply:** Aramco/Sinopec build-out vs. European cracker closures (IEA: EU petrochemical feedstock demand −19% 2019–24) [^23^] and Nexant's warning that COTC scale "can and would strongly alter the world's petrochemical balance and prices." [^67^]
5. **Conversions: climate asset or life-extension of stranded assets?** Industry frames conversions as transition delivery (P66: "advances… long-held strategy to expand our renewable fuels production" [^42^]); EJ advocates frame them as deferral of decommissioning/remediation [^46^]; economics remain policy-credit-dependent with demonstrated reversal risk [^47^][^60^].
6. **Scenario–strategy firewall:** Shell scenarios are explicitly "not… expressions of Shell's strategy or business plan" [^57^] — companies get the reputational benefit of net-zero scenarios (Horizon) without an accountability bridge to plans.
7. **Scope-1+2 roadmaps vs. Scope-3 reality:** refining emits ~1 GtCO₂e/yr directly [^9^], but refined-product combustion is ~5–10× that; OGCI/CATF roadmaps address the former, Carbon Tracker/TPI judge companies on the latter. [^38^][^39^]

## 8. IMPLICATIONS FOR VCO / CI KNOWLEDGE MANAGEMENT (analyst synthesis)

1. **Treat the backcast as a living artifact, not a publication.** Evidence shows pathways decay (targets retired at the planning boundary; pathways updated only under investor pressure). Adopt DAPP-style signposts/triggers per milestone (e.g., carbon price, low-carbon power availability, 45Z/LCFS credit value, SAF blend-limit approvals, COTC market balance) with pre-agreed responses.[^48^][^49^]
2. **Anchor every >10-year target to an owned milestone *inside* the current planning period.** The Shell/BP record shows unowned, out-of-horizon targets are disposable. CA100+/Carbon Tracker hallmarks (interim absolute targets; full life-cycle coverage) provide an external benchmark.[^38^]
3. **Couple the strategic backcast to refinery planning tools.** Site roadmaps (Worley two-phase method; CATF archetype LP modeling) are where end states meet LP economics, hydrogen balance, and turnaround cycles; the CI system should capture the assumption set behind each option (cost, TRL, dependencies) so lookbacks can test them.[^11^][^13^]
4. **Institutionalize lookback cadence.** Quist et al. show follow-up is possible but not automatic; schedule 2–3-year pathway re-baselining (as Shell does for scenarios, 2023→2025→2026) *with* a documented audit trail of what changed and why — converting strategy resets into double-loop learning rather than credibility loss.[^7^][^30^]
5. **Exploit low-regret intermediate steps as "options with triggers":** co-processing (≤5% now; ASTM 30% under evaluation), e-boilers/e-drives (<$150/t), H₂-pinch — each cheap today, scalable on defined triggers; this is the pragmatic bridge between a 2050 vision and next year's budget.[^21^][^9^]
6. **Watch the end-state substitution risk in portfolio backcasts:** COTC and LNG growth can function as demand hedges that preserve hydrocarbon throughput rather than decarbonize it; CI knowledge base should record which end state each asset decision serves (decarbonization vs. demand-pivot vs. exit) to keep portfolio logic auditable.[^17^][^35^]
