1. What this workbook is and when to use it

The workbook is a structured interviewing and capture tool. Its job is to build a complete, plain-language picture of the knowledge the business already produces: every report, review, matrix, spreadsheet, meeting note, logbook, email thread, and piece of hard-won experience, together with where each one lives, who uses it, what it is used for, and what happens to it afterward.

The teams already run lookbacks (plan vs actual), backcasts (actual vs best possible), post-audits, maturity assessments, and regular continuous improvement (CI) reporting. That material already exists. It is scattered across systems, formats, folders, and people. This workbook captures that landscape so the future enterprise knowledge management capability is designed around what is real, not what is assumed.

Use it when you need to:

  • prepare for building a knowledge management capability (inventory first, design second);
  • collect requirements from the business without overwhelming people (the work is broken into Coverage plus six small modules);
  • create a defensible evidence base (real items, real pain points, real people at risk) for investment decisions.
The golden rule Inventory before design. Resist proposing tools or platforms while capturing. The workbook records what exists and what is needed, not what should be bought.

2. The fill order at a glance

The workbook is modular. Do not fill every sheet on day one. Run one step, absorb the results, then run the next. Coverage is a living list, not a module you finish once.

Workbook Stages 0-4 are the order you fill the sheets. That is not the same as Horizon on M6, which is when we would build a need (first release, later, or future).

Two sheets are reference only: Glossary (every term, plus the M1 checklist) and Eight gaps (the eight problems this work is meant to close). Tag M5 and M6 rows to a gap when you can.

Modules can be reused later. Repeat M1 for a new site, or refresh M4 as people rotate. The sheets are independent. IDs link them: P- outputs, S- systems, K- people, PP- pains, N- needs. Never invent a second scheme.

3. How to fill in any module

Every module sheet works the same way:

  1. Read the beige row under the column titles. It says what belongs in that column, in plain words.
  2. Look at the grey EXAMPLE rows. Copy that style. Then overwrite or delete them.
  3. Use the dropdowns. Pick the closest option. Use Notes if nothing fits.
  4. Give everything an ID. P-01 for items, S-01 for systems, K-01 for people, PP-01 for pain points, N-01 for needs. Other sheets point at these IDs. Keep each ID unique.
  5. Do not wait for perfect. A rough row today is better than a precise row that never gets written. "Don't know" is a valid dropdown choice.
  6. Tick Coverage when a site or team is done for that module, so you know when to stop interviewing.
Tip Open Glossary first if a column header is unclear. The checklist at the bottom of Glossary is for M1: tick what exists, add anything missing.

4. Module-by-module instructions

Coverage - who we have talked to

One row per site and team (example: Site A / Planning). Tick a module when that group is done. This is how you know Stage 1 (or 2, 3, 4) is actually finished, not just started. Do not mark Done from memory of "we think we covered that."

  • Who runs it: the project lead. It is a living list.
  • How: add a row when you start a site or team. Update the module dropdowns after each session.
  • Watch for: a module marked Done with nobody named in "Who we spoke to."

M1 Produced - what is already produced

The foundation. One row per output the work produces: lookback matrices, margin bridges, backcast reports, post-audits, CI reports, minutes, logbooks, decision emails. Do not judge quality yet.

  • Who runs it: the people who produce the outputs (CI team, planners, schedulers, controllers), in 1-2 short sessions.
  • How: work through the checklist at the bottom of Glossary. For each item capture what it is, which activity produces it, how often, in what tool and format, where it is stored, and who owns it.
  • Two separate columns: Is it complete? (is the content up to date and filled in?) is not the same as Can we trust it to reuse? (how much should the next person trust it: approved, reviewed, as-captured, raw notes).
  • Watch for: no clear owner; the same report in several places; items only the producer can find (the sheet flags these).
  • Output: an inventory with P- IDs. M3 and M6 point here.

M2 Systems - where things live

One row per system or major storage place. "Searchable" means a normal user can find a specific past item in a few minutes.

  • Who runs it: IT plus one regular user per system.
  • How: one row per system. What it is used for, which M1 items it holds (cite P- IDs), who can open it, how searchable it is, and how long content is kept.
  • Watch for: systems only a few people can search; knowledge locked in mailboxes or vendor formats; "no rule" retention.
  • Output: the systems landscape with S- IDs. Later it defines where the knowledge capability must connect.

M3 How used - the purpose test

For each M1 item, record who uses it, what for, which process step it feeds, and what happens next. Does a finding change a plan, a model, a procedure, or a decision, or is it filed and forgotten? Is follow-up ever checked?

Type a P- ID. The name fills from M1 automatically. If the name is blank, the ID is missing or mistyped.

The four closures are the intended landings for a finding. A finding is not done until one of these is true and checked:

  1. Finding updates the planning model.
  2. Lesson becomes a procedure or standard.
  3. Finding updates the next plan or pathway.
  4. A person's know-how is written into the system.

Pick None of these if it does not land anywhere. That is a useful answer.

  • Who runs it: the people who use the outputs (often not the producers).
  • Watch for: "What is hard to reconstruct later" - assumptions, alternatives, who decided. That missing context is what the future system must capture.
  • Output: the usage and follow-up map, including which closure (if any) each item supports.

M4 People - know-how that is not written down

Identifies critical knowledge that exists only in people's heads (planning judgment, workarounds, pattern recognition) and the exposure if those people rotate, retire, or leave. Each row gets a K- ID.

  • Who runs it: short interviews with managers and experienced staff. Roles can replace names.
  • How: for each person or role, capture what they know that is not written down, where it shows up in decisions, and who else has it. Then split the risk into three columns: How big is the risk (High / Medium / Low if they leave tomorrow), Why it is at risk (single holder, retirement, rotation, contractor), and When they could leave or rotate (best estimate).
  • Watch for: "Nobody" as the backup; High risk with capture = No.
  • Output: a prioritized list of people to interview or shadow before knowledge walks out the door. M6 can cite K- IDs.

M5 Pains - where it hurts today

Write it as you would tell a colleague. Stories and rough numbers both count. These rows become the case for change and the evidence behind M6.

  • Who runs it: a mixed group of people who feel the pain. Capture every example raised.
  • How: one row per pain point (PP-), with a real story wherever possible. Tag Which research gap from the Eight gaps sheet when you can. Pick Other if it does not fit. If M5 and M6 have no rows tagged to a gap, that gap is still open.
  • Watch for: the same pain type across sites. Recurring patterns are stronger evidence than one-off anecdotes. Gaps 4 (assumptions) and 5 (KPI names) are the ones people forget.
  • Output: the evidence base for the business case and for M6.

M6 Needs - what the capability must do

Turns the evidence into a prioritized statement of what the future knowledge capability must do, written as outcomes ("anyone can find the last turnaround review in under a minute"), not as tools or products.

  • Who runs it: the sponsor plus the people who ran the earlier modules. Last, once M1-M5 have evidence.
  • How: one row per need (N-). Cite at least one PP-, P-, or K- ID. If you cannot, question the need. Set Priority (Must / Should / Could / Not now) and Horizon (first release, later, or future with AI). Horizon is not workbook Stage 1-4.
  • Also tag: which research gap it closes (optional), and which of the four closures it supports if the need is about landing a finding.
  • Watch for: a need that does not trace back to a captured pain, item, or person.
  • Output: the agreed, evidence-backed requirements baseline for design.

5. Keeping the workbook tidy

  • One master copy. Nominate a single owner. Circulate exports for reading, not parallel copies for editing. The current file is CI-KM-discovery-workbook_V2.xlsx.
  • IDs are permanent. Never reuse or renumber P-, S-, K-, PP-, or N- IDs. Other sheets reference them.
  • Replace examples early. The grey EXAMPLE rows are scaffolding. Remove them once real entries exist.
  • Filter, don't sort wildly. Each module has filters and frozen headers. Use filters for views rather than reordering rows others are referencing.
  • Tick Coverage as you go. That is how you know a site or team is done for a module.
  • Version by date. Save a dated copy at the end of a fill-order stage (for example CI-KM-discovery-workbook_V2_2026-09.xlsx) so you can show how the picture evolved.

6. What happens after capture

The completed workbook is not the end product. It is the design input. Coverage shows who was actually interviewed. The inventory (M1-M2) defines what must be migrated and connected, including what is complete vs what is safe to reuse. The usage map (M3) defines the capture templates and the four closures. The people map (M4) defines who to elicit first. The pain points (M5), tagged to the eight gaps, define the business case. The needs (M6), each with a Horizon, become the ratified requirements baseline.

The companion document - From Discovery to Enterprise Knowledge Management: The Build Roadmap - describes this path in full: how each module's output maps to a component of the future capability, the build sequence, governance, how success is measured, and how the captured data shapes enterprise search over past decisions and their consequences.

Why each module matters downstream

Every module is a design input. The one-line answer for each:

  • Coverage becomes the rollout map: which sites and teams are done, and who is still missing.
  • M1 Produced becomes the ingestion catalogue: what content must be brought in, from where, whether it is complete, and whether it is safe to reuse.
  • M2 Systems becomes the connection plan: one connection method per source, and what can be copied out versus read in place.
  • M3 How used becomes the search requirements and the four closures: what was decided, what happened next, and whether a finding actually landed.
  • M4 People becomes the validation loop and the elicitation order: who reviews content, and whose know-how must be captured first (K- IDs).
  • M5 Pains becomes the priority use cases, tagged to the eight research gaps.
  • M6 Needs becomes the requirements baseline, sequenced by Horizon, not by workbook fill-order stage.

The Build Roadmap turns these inputs into a plain-language, tool-agnostic target architecture (ingestion, distillation, a small shared vocabulary, retrieval, and access) in "Designing the architecture: from captured data to enterprise search".
