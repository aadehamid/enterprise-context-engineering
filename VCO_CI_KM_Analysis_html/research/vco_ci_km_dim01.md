# KEY FINDINGS (Summary)

1. **The downstream chain is a margin-squeezing machine between two volatile prices.** A refiner's economics are the spread between a globally-priced crude input and locally/seasonally-priced product outputs. Crude is the dominant cost — about **85% of a typical refining operating cost is crude oil** [^37^], and CRS notes *"the greatest cost in refining is not capital investment, but crude costs."* [^4^] Every structural and operational decision is therefore aimed at maximizing the gross-to-net margin cascade: **Gross margin → Net margin (– variable cost) → Cash margin (– fixed cost)** [^18^].

2. **Crude/feedstock selection is the single largest value lever.** Crudes are priced as *benchmark ± differential*; the differential reflects *"the cost to get a given crude to the refiner and the value of its yield."* [^5^] Light/heavy (sweet/sour) differentials are typically **15–25% of the light-sweet price** [^6^] and swung from +$15.51/bbl (2006) to **–$3.64/bbl (2011, inverted)** as heavy-crude demand for complex refineries shifted [^4^]. Valuation is via **Gross Product Worth (GPW)** and **netback** (GPW – transport – processing) [^7^][^8^].

3. **Refinery configuration (Nelson Complexity Index) determines what crude can be run and what slate can be made.** Complexity rises from Topping (1) → Hydroskimming (2) → Cracking (5) → Coking (9+); the US average is ~10 and Gulf Coast conversion refineries run 12–13, yielding ~8% jet and >30% diesel [^4^][^13^][^14^]. The CDU/VDU separate crude; FCC, hydrocracker, coker, reformer and alkylation convert/upgrade; hydrotreaters and the hydrogen (SMR) plant enable clean-fuel specs [^10^].

4. **Margin structures are cyclical and have re-rated.** The benchmark 3-2-1 crack spread averaged **just over $17/bbl (2007–2020) but $29.50/bbl (2021–2025)** after 3–4 mb/d of capacity closed 2020–2023 [^17^]. Trade press put the 2025 average at $12–15/bbl vs a 2022 peak of ~$35/bbl and a 10-yr average of $8–10/bbl [^22^]. Refining margins can range from **–$2 to +$20/bbl** [^20^]. Operating costs of an ultra-complex refinery are ~**$7.6/bbl** (variable $0.7, fixed $2.6, capital $4.3) [^19^].

5. **Energy is the largest controllable operating cost.** Energy is ~**60% of EU refinery cash operating costs** (doubled in 20 years) [^33^]; an efficient 100 kb/d refinery burns ~5% of feed as energy vs ~8% for an inefficient one (a ~$15M/yr gap) [^35^]. Within energy use, fired heaters are 74–78%, steam 18–20%, power 4–6% [^34^]. This links the operations decision directly to both margin and Scope 1/2 emissions.

6. **Logistics is a distinct, costed layer and a strategic asset.** The US alone has a **95,000-mile product-pipeline network** [^4^]; crude-by-rail "all-in" costs ran **$8.54–$13.00/bbl** depending on destination [^25^]. In the retail pump price, **distribution & marketing ≈ 10–16%** (vs crude 54–56%, refining 14–19%, taxes 13–17%) [^23^]. Players actively manage "secondary logistics costs" and tariffs as a profit lever [^54^].

7. **Marketing is where thin fuel margins meet higher-margin non-fuel and channels.** Retail fuel gross margins are razor-thin (net ~**$0.13/gal** [^27^]; EBIT ~$0.17/gal [^28^]; net profit 1–2% [^29^]); profit is made in-store (non-fuel ~39% of revenue but **63% of gross profit** [^29^]). Channels span wholesale/rack, retail, aviation (into-plane), marine bunker (VLSFO, ISO 8217), petrochemicals, and export [^3^][^32^].

8. **The strategic center of gravity is shifting from fuels to petrochemicals and from West to East.** IEA: petrochemicals become the **dominant source of oil-demand growth from 2026**, consuming **18.4 mb/d (more than 1 in 6 barrels) by 2030** [^39^]; refinery-supplied naphtha's share of petchem feedstock fell from 57% (2010) to 45% (2024) as NGLs surge [^38^]. Refined-product demand **peaks ~2027 at 86.3 mb/d**; **East of Suez refining capacity overtakes the Atlantic Basin by 2026** [^38^].

9. **Overcapacity + energy transition = structural margin pressure and closures.** IEA sees **4.2 mb/d of new capacity vs 1.6 mb/d of closures by 2030**, with excess capacity rising to **11.4 mb/d by 2030**; high-cost Europe and US West Coast are most exposed [^38^]. McKinsey's reference case sees the **global refining value pool shrink ~35% to ~$100 bn by the 2030s**, with US/Europe needing ~5 mb/d of closures by 2035 [^40^].

10. **VCO and lookback/backcasting are the integrative discipline that captures the spread.** Real-world VCO organizations (Marathon, Chevron, Phillips 66) exist to *"identify value chain constraints and opportunities, determine the economic impact, and develop solutions to mitigate and capture."* [^46^] Industry estimates put end-to-end VCO value at **$30–85M/yr for a mid-sized refiner**, with yield/slate optimization worth **$0.50–$1.00/bbl**, and explicitly cite **"performance back casting"** as an organizational enabler [^44^]. A persistent **plan-vs-actual margin gap** ("margin leakage") is the core target for lookback analytics and CI [^45^].

# SECTION 2 — CRUDE & FEEDSTOCK PROCUREMENT AND TRADING

## 2.1 Benchmarks, quality and differentials

### Claim 2.1 — Crudes are priced off a few benchmarks (Brent, WTI, Dubai/Oman) at a differential that captures quality and logistics; fundamentally "the cost to get a given crude to the refiner and the value of its yield."
- **Source:** Crowdfield, "Crude Oil Pricing: A Guide to How the Market Actually Works"
- **URL:** https://www.crowdfield.net/blogposts/crude-oil-pricing-a-guide-to-how-the-market-actually-works
- **Date:** undated
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Every other crude oil in the world is typically priced at a differential to one of these benchmarks. These differentials account for quality, transportation, and regional factors... A rule of thumb: light, sweet crudes sell at a premium; heavy or sour crudes sell at a discount... Fundamentally, price differentials reflect the cost to get a given crude to the refiner and the value of its yield." And: "Brent... used to price roughly 60–70% of crude oil traded... WTI... often trades $2–$6 below Brent due to transportation and logistical costs."
- **Context:** The differential mechanism is the raw material of crude procurement/trading decisions — buyers arbitrage quality vs. delivered cost.
- **Confidence:** High (concept); Medium (specific $ figures, time-varying).

### Claim 2.2 — Crude has no intrinsic value; value derives from the products it can yield. Light/heavy differential typically 15–25% of the light-sweet price and widens with oil price.
- **Source:** ScienceDirect (Heavy Oil / Host Government overview, "How crude oil obtains its value")
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/heavy-oil
- **Date:** undated
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Crude oil... has no value in its natural form, but its value derives from the products it can help create and the proportion of the higher value products (typically the transportation fuels) that an individual crude oil can yield... the differential between light sweet crude (higher quality) and heavy sour crude (lower quality) will widen with increasing global oil prices. Typically, the differential range is between 15% and 25% of the price of light sweet crude."
- **Context:** This is the theoretical basis for GPW/netback valuation and for complex-refinery "upgrading" economics.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 2.3 — Quality differentials are quantified by API gravity and sulfur; light/sweet can trade at $10+/bbl premiums to medium/sour; freight is a live input.
- **Source:** Discovery Alert, "Current Crude Oil Prices: Market Trends in 2025"
- **URL:** https://discoveryalert.com.au/news/current-crude-oil-prices-2025-market-trends/
- **Date:** 2025-04-16
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Quality differentials related to API gravity and sulfur content explain the $5–8 price disparities between light/sweet and heavy/sour crudes... Shipping costs play a significant role in benchmark differentials, with Brent transport from the North Sea to Asia costing approximately $2.50/bbl versus $4.20/bbl for WTI from the Gulf Coast."
- **Context:** Gives concrete, recent magnitudes for quality premia and freight — the two levers a crude trader optimizes.
- **Confidence:** Medium (single trade-press source, time-stamped).

### Claim 2.4 — Heavy Canadian grades (WCS/AWB) trade at a differential below WTI, driven by quality and pipeline egress.
- **Source:** MEG Energy, 2024 Year-End MD&A
- **URL:** http://www.megenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/MEG-Energy-Corp_Year-End-2024-Report-to-Shareholders_Final.pdf
- **Date:** 2025 (FY2024 report)
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "WCS typically trades at a differential below the WTI benchmark price and is impacted by apportionment levels on pipelines leaving the Edmonton market... WCS and AWB differentials improved in 2024, relative to 2023, reflecting sustained global demand for heavy crude and the unconstrained egress enabled by the TMX pipeline start-up."
- **Context:** Shows that differentials are not static — they respond to logistics (egress) and heavy-crude demand, a key trading/procurement dynamic.
- **Confidence:** High (issuer MD&A).

## 2.2 Light/heavy spread dynamics and the value of complexity

### Claim 2.5 — The light/heavy crude spread is highly cyclical and can invert; it is the return to conversion capacity.
- **Source:** Congressional Research Service (CRS), R41478, "The U.S. Oil Refining Industry: Background in Changing Markets and Fuel Policies"
- **URL:** https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41478.html
- **Date:** ~2012 (report; data through 2012)
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Table 3. Light/Heavy Crude Oil Price Spread ($/Bbl): 2006 $15.51; 2007 $12.88; 2008 $14.85; 2009 $5.60; 2010 $9.12; 2011 -$3.64... the price premium became negative in May 2011 with Mexican Maya (a heavy crude) commanding a higher price than WTI. During the first eight months of 2012, the price spread remained inverted with Maya priced over $5.71 higher, on average, than WTI." And: "During the period of high oil prices from 2004 through 2008, heavy crude oils sold at a large discount relative to light crude. The relative tightness in the light crude market, coupled with the price discounts for heavy crude, induced refiners to invest in facilities and processes that would make refineries more able to process heavy crude oils... These investments declined in profitability after oil prices fell and the price premium narrowed and inverted in 2011."
- **Context:** A decisive decision-economics lesson: conversion (coker) investments are a bet on a wide light/heavy spread; when the spread inverts, the capital under-performs. **Counter-view/tension:** complexity is valuable only when the spread rewards it.
- **Confidence:** High (CRS, data-backed).

## 2.3 Valuation: Gross Product Worth (GPW) and Netback

### Claim 2.6 — Crudes are valued by Gross Product Worth (yield × regional product price), and the market-clearing value is set by the "marginal configuration."
- **Source:** Netback Impact Analysis of West Coast Export Capacity (Wood Mackenzie, for IAAC Canada)
- **URL:** https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/54588/54588E.pdf
- **Date:** undated (regulatory filing)
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Refining Value Analysis, which is the value of products produced from a given crude oil (i.e., GPW or Gross Product Worth), is central to our approach. Individual refiners choose crudes providing the highest margin for their particular configuration... Gross Product Worth: Revenue of a refinery determined by the yield of each product multiplied by its regional product price... Marginal configuration: represents the refinery configuration that sets the value of the crude as it is the configuration in which the last barrel of that crude is processed."
- **Context:** GPW is the refinery-specific value of a crude; the marginal barrel sets the differential. This is the core of crude selection and of lookback ("did we buy the right crude?").
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 2.7 — Netback = GPW minus transport and processing; a worked example shows the arithmetic.
- **Source:** Netback Impact Analysis of West Coast Export Capacity (Wood Mackenzie, for IAAC Canada)
- **URL:** https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/54588/54588E.pdf
- **Date:** undated
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "For example, if the USGC refining value of Cold Lake is $C 80.75/bbl, then the netback in Edmonton would be the USGC value, less transportation (C$6.80/bbl), less the difference in refinery operating costs relative to Arab Heavy, the competing heavy crude oil, (C$1.00). Thus the C$80.75/bbl netbacks to C$72.95/bbl in Edmonton."
- **Context:** The explicit netback formula links crude value at the refinery gate back to a producer/refiner location — the everyday calculation of procurement and trading desks.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 2.8 — The "Verleger hypothesis": crude is valued as a weighted average of refined product prices, less transport and processing (netback pricing).
- **Source:** Aalto University thesis, "Predicting Crude Oil Market Value Based on Crude Oil [Properties]"
- **URL:** https://sal.aalto.fi/publications/pdf-files/theses/bac/tkau20_public.pdf
- **Date:** undated (2020)
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Verleger (1982) states that crude oils are valued on the market based on the value of refined products... The value of crude oil is a weighted average of the principal products refined from crude oil, deducted with transportation costs and processing costs. This follows from the netback pricing formula in which transportation costs and refining margin is deducted from gross product worth."
- **Context:** Academic grounding tying product prices → crude value. Asche et al. (2003) add nuance: crude drives product prices in the long run, but products can influence crude in the short run. **Counter-view:** direction of causality is debated.
- **Confidence:** High (academic synthesis).

# SECTION 3 — REFINING OPERATIONS (CDU/VDU → CONVERSION → TREATING → BLENDING → UTILITIES/HYDROGEN/ENERGY)

## 3.1 The process flow: separation, conversion, treatment

### Claim 3.1 — Refining comprises three functions: separation (distillation), yield improvement (conversion), and treatment to meet specs/compliance.
- **Source:** CATF, "Refinery of the Future" (archetype selection study)
- **URL:** https://cdn.catf.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/29181306/refinery-of-the-future-archetype-selection.pdf
- **Date:** 2024
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "The refining process essentially comprises separation, yield improvement(conversion) and treatment processes through a variety of process units. Crude is separated into various hydrocarbon component ranges through boiling and thereafter converted/conditioned further by catalytic means. Yield improvement(conversion) relates to maximisation of high value products(e.g., shift molecules to diesel rather than fuel oil). Treatment of specific streams are used to get to final product specification(e.g., sulphur, octane) or to ensure environmental compliance."
- **Context:** The cleanest one-line functional model of a refinery for decision purposes: separate → convert → treat.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 3.2 — The CDU/VDU are the primary separation units; they set nameplate capacity and produce the straight-run fractions.
- **Source:** CATF, "Refinery of the Future"
- **URL:** https://cdn.catf.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/29181306/refinery-of-the-future-archetype-selection.pdf
- **Date:** 2024
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "The primary units in the refinery are the CDU and VDU which separates the crude into products, by distillation, ranging from light materials such as LPG, naphtha, etc. to middle distillates such as jet, kerosene, diesel and the heavier material into gasoil and residue."
- **Context:** CDU (atmospheric) + VDU (vacuum) define throughput and the raw slate fed to conversion units.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 3.3 — Unit-by-unit roles: FCC→gasoline; hydrocracker→diesel/jet; coker→resid destruction; reformer→octane/hydrogen; alkylation→high-octane blendstock; hydrotreaters→desulfurization.
- **Source:** CATF, "Refinery of the Future"; corroborated by CRS R41478 Appendix B and IJET
- **URL:** https://cdn.catf.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/29181306/refinery-of-the-future-archetype-selection.pdf ; https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41478.html
- **Date:** 2024 (CATF); ~2012 (CRS)
- **Excerpt (verbatim, CATF):** "The FCC converts Atmospheric Residue from CDU, i.e., the heavier lesser valuable portions of crude, into gasoline components... a Hydrocracker for conversion of heavier gasoil products from VDU... produces majority of the diesel pool... and provides the refinery with flexibility to switch operations between gasoline and diesel... Alkylation, where lighter products from FCC such as butylenes, are converted into alkylates which can then be blended into the gasoline pool, hence increasing the profitability of the refinery."
- **Excerpt (verbatim, CRS):** "Fluid catalytic cracking uses high temperature and catalysts to convert heavy gas oil mostly into gasoline. Hydrocracking uses catalysts to react gas oil and hydrogen under high pressure and high temperature to make both jet fuel and gasoline. Coking converts low-value residuum (using thermal-cracking) to high-value light products, producing petroleum coke as a by-product... a reforming unit uses precious-metal catalysts (platinum and rhenium) to 'reform' hydrocarbon molecules into high-octane gasoline components. The reforming process removes hydrogen from low-octane gasoline, which refinery reuses in various cracking (hydrocracking) and treating (hydrotreating) units."
- **Context:** Establishes each conversion unit's value role and the flexibility (e.g., hydrocracker gasoline/diesel swing) that optimization exploits.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Claim 3.4 — Hydrogen (via Steam Methane Reforming) and the fuel/energy system are the enabling utilities for clean fuels; off-gases + natural gas feed the SMR.
- **Source:** CATF, "Refinery of the Future"
- **URL:** https://cdn.catf.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/29181306/refinery-of-the-future-archetype-selection.pdf
- **Date:** 2024
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "The Hydrodesulphurisation and Hydrocracker units consume Hydrogen. This is generated internally by the Steam Methane Reformer(SMR) unit. The lightest components from the CDU, ie mainly Methane, Ethane and other lighters, called Refinery Off Gases, that cannot be sold as products directly, can be used as feedstock to the SMR to produce Hydrogen, natural gas can be imported as make up... The combination of Refinery Off gases and natural gas also meet the internal fuel requirements of the refinery."
- **Context:** Hydrogen is a binding constraint and a major cost/emissions source; the hydrogen balance and the fuel-gas system are core decision variables (make vs. buy H2, off-gas vs. natural gas).
- **Confidence:** High.

## 3.3 Energy, utilities and the operating-cost structure

### Claim 3.9 — Energy is ~60% of EU refinery cash operating costs, a share that doubled over 20 years; energy use rises with complexity.
- **Source:** Concawe, "EU refinery energy systems and efficiency" (Rpt 12-03)
- **URL:** https://www.concawe.eu/wp-content/uploads/rpt_12-03-2012-01520-01-e.pdf
- **Date:** 2012
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Energy currently accounts for about 60% of the cash operating costs of EU refineries, a proportion that has doubled over the last 20 years as a result of increasingly stringent product specifications, higher refinery complexity and fast increasing energy costs... The absolute energy consumption of a refinery is not only determined by the amount of material it processes... but also, and to a major extent, by its complexity, mostly represented by the amount of 'conversion' of heavy streams into lighter products."
- **Context:** The pivotal operating-cost fact: energy is the largest controllable cost and is structurally tied to complexity and to emissions. (Dated 2012, but directionally still cited as the standard figure.)
- **Confidence:** High (industry body); note age.

# SECTION 4 — PRODUCT SLATE OPTIMIZATION (GPW, crack spreads, margin cascade)

## 4.1 The margin cascade: gross → net → cash

### Claim 4.1 — Refining profitability is a cascade: Gross margin = Σ(product price × yield) – crude; Net = Gross – variable cost; Cash = Net – fixed cost.
- **Source:** Fahim, Al-Sahhaf & Elkilani, "Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining" (textbook, ch. 16.4)
- **URL:** https://uomosul.edu.iq/public/files/datafolder_2977/_20201029_111223_110.pdf (mirror: https://oilworldtoday.files.wordpress.com/2017/10/fundamentals-of-petroleum-refining.pdf)
- **Date:** 2010 (textbook)
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "The gross margin is the difference between the composite value of the refined products at the refinery gate and the cost of the crude oil delivered to the refinery: Gross margin = Σ (price of product i × yield of product i) − crude price... The net refining margin is the gross margin less the variable refining costs, that is, chemicals, catalysts, fuel and working capital charges: Net margin = gross margin − variable cost... the difference between the net margin and the fixed cost per barrel of crude oil is called the cash margin... Cash margin = net margin − fixed cost."
- **Context:** The canonical decision-economics equations of the entire study. Every operational and commercial decision moves one of these terms (yield/price, variable cost, fixed cost).
- **Confidence:** High (standard textbook).

### Claim 4.2 — Operating cost of a typical ultra-complex refinery ≈ $7.6/bbl (variable $0.7, fixed $2.6, capital $4.3).
- **Source:** Aalto University thesis, "A Multi-Objective Linear Programming Model for Ranking Competing Refineries" (adapted from Fahim et al., 2010)
- **URL:** https://sal.aalto.fi/publications/pdf-files/tlin18_public.pdf
- **Date:** 2018
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "Operating costs of a typical ultra-complex refinery... Variable cost $4/ton ($0.7/bbl); Fixed cost $15/ton ($2.6/bbl); Cost of capital $25/ton ($4.3/bbl); Total $44/ton ($7.6/bbl)." And: "Refinery costs are affected by the refinery's location, size, and complexity as well as crude quality, capacity utilization, and environmental constraints."
- **Context:** A concrete per-barrel cost stack that, set against the crack spread, shows how thin the true economic margin can be after capital.
- **Confidence:** High (academic, sourced to Fahim).

### Claim 4.3 — Crude dominates cost: about 85% of a typical refining operating cost is crude oil; GRM is the value gap between products and crude.
- **Source:** Academy of Business (AB Academies), "Crack Spread Forecasting for Supply Chain Optimization" (citing Robinson, 2007; Dunis et al., 2016)
- **URL:** https://www.abacademies.org/articles/crack-spread-forecasting-for-supply-chain-optimization-a-hybrid-model-using-time-series-and-deep-learning-with-bayesian-optimizati-16510.html
- **Date:** 2023-12-14
- **Excerpt (verbatim):** "About 85% (Robinson, 2007) of a typical refining operating cost is made up of crude oil. Because of this, refiners and non-integrated marketers face a significant risk from rising crude oil prices and falling refined product prices on both sides of the market... refiners... model and predict crack spread with the objectives... of (a) Profit maximization by deciding when to procure stocks of crude or processed products (b) Increasing GRM (Gross Refining Margin... the difference between the cost of the crude oil used as the input and the total value of the petroleum products...) and (c) Implementing low risk trading / hedging against volatility in crude price fluctuations through futures contracts for protecting the margins."
- **Context:** Explains why crude procurement + hedging dominate decision economics, and why crack-spread forecasting is a core VCO capability.
- **Confidence:** High (peer-reviewed, well-cited).

# SECTION 9 — COUNTER-VIEWS & TENSIONS (explicitly noted)

1. **Complexity pays — until it doesn't.** Conversion (coking) capital is a bet on a wide light/heavy spread. CRS shows the spread inverting to –$3.64/bbl in 2011, after which "these investments declined in profitability." [^4^] Yet ScienceDirect and MEG show heavy/sour discounts of 15–25% as the norm that rewards complexity [^6^][^57^]. Tension: the value of complexity is regime-dependent.

2. **Crack-spread level depends on the series and window.** $17/bbl (2007–2020, CME 3-2-1) [^17^] vs $8–10/bbl "10-year average" [^22^] vs $12–15/bbl (2025) [^22^] vs ~$35/bbl (2022 peak) [^22^]. These are different windows/regions/definitions; use ranges, not point estimates. EIA stresses the crack "exclude[s] refining costs other than crude." [^15^]

3. **Margin outlook is scenario-dependent.** McKinsey's 2030s value pool spans $40 bn (accelerated transition) → $100 bn (reference) → $181 bn (delayed) [^40^]. IEA is more sanguine (tepid demand, overcapacity) [^38^], while FuelsEurope (advocacy) frames a €400–650 bn low-carbon growth pathway [^50^]. Treat all as scenarios, not forecasts.

4. **Petchem growth helps refiners — and bypasses them.** IEA: petchem feedstocks drive oil-demand growth, but NGLs (not refinery naphtha) increasingly supply them (57%→45% refinery share) [^38^]. Digital Refining quotes IEA that "the petrochemical path can offer higher margins than fuels" for *integrated* refiners [^62^] — i.e., the benefit accrues only to those who integrate.

5. **Crude drives product prices (long run) vs. products drive crude (short run).** Verleger/netback says crude is valued off products [^8^]; Asche et al. find crude exogenous long-run but products can move crude short-run [^8^]. Direction of causality matters for lookback attribution.

6. **Returns vs. strategic motives.** IOCs divest downstream for returns (ADL) [^43^]; NOCs invest for security/industrialization [^43^]. Same asset, different objective function — different "optimal" decisions.

7. **Retail: fuel is a traffic driver, not the profit center.** Fuel = 61% of revenue but non-fuel = 63% of gross profit [^29^]; net fuel margin ~1–2% / ~$0.13/gal [^27^][^29^]. Marketing optimization is therefore as much about in-store/loyalty as about the fuel molecule.

8. **"Back casting" evidence is thin in public sources.** The term appears verbatim in a consultancy [^44^], and the plan-vs-actual "margin leakage" gap is well documented [^45^], but rigorous public methodology for refinery lookback/CI knowledge management is largely proprietary (e.g., inside LP/vendor systems [^41^][^47^]). This is a genuine evidence gap (see Gaps).
